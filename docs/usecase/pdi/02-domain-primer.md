# PDI — Domain Primer

**Purpose:** the concepts below are load-bearing for everything else in this document set — almost every downstream design decision in PDI (what gets matched, what gets extracted, how things get rated) depends on understanding these terms first. This primer is written for a business audience, not a legal one; see `06-open-questions-and-risks.md` for where legal nuance becomes an active open decision.

## The core business context: self-funded employer health plans

PDI operates entirely within the world of **self-funded employee health plans governed by ERISA** (the Employee Retirement Income Security Act). In a self-funded arrangement, the *employer* — not an insurance company — actually pays employee medical claims out of its own money. A **TPA (third-party administrator)** handles the paperwork and claims processing, but the employer carries the financial risk. This is different from a "fully insured" plan, where an insurance carrier bears the risk and the employer just pays a premium.

**Why this matters for PDI's entire premise:** because the employer bears the risk directly, **the plan document itself is the single most consequential piece of paper in the relationship** — it determines what's covered, how much gets paid for out-of-network care, how the plan recovers money when someone else caused an injury, and how disputes get decided. Weak or missing language in that one document is a direct, quantifiable financial and legal exposure to the employer — and that exposure is exactly what PDI is built to find and monetize (by pointing the employer toward the paid services that fix it).

## The document family PDI has to work with

| Document | What it is | Why it matters to PDI |
|---|---|---|
| **Plan Document (PD)** | The actual governing instrument — eligibility, benefits, exclusions, claims/appeals procedures, subrogation rights, plan administration. For self-funded plans this is often combined with the SPD into one document. | This is the primary thing PDI reads and rates. |
| **Summary Plan Description (SPD)** | The participant-facing disclosure ERISA requires. Sometimes a separate document from the PD. | **A PD/SPD conflict is itself a finding** — courts have ruled both ways when the two disagree, so a real mismatch is a real, reportable compliance exposure, not just an internal inconsistency to quietly resolve. |
| **Wrap Document** | A document that adds ERISA-required language around an insurance certificate or benefit summary that's missing it (common when a TPA/carrier's own paperwork is incomplete). Also refers to "wrap networks" — broad rental networks layered on top of a primary network. | A signal of how the plan's overall documentation was assembled. |
| **Amendments** | Changes to the plan document, often effective mid-year. | **This is the single most important structural nuance in the whole system**: an assessment run only against the base, restated plan document is *wrong* if a later amendment changed the relevant language. PDI has to resolve which document actually "controls" a given provision as of the date being assessed — see `03-deliverable-1-intelligence-engine.md` §D1-S2. |
| **Form 5500** | The annual filing every ERISA plan must submit to the Department of Labor. Identified by the employer's EIN plus a 3-digit Plan Number. **Schedule A** discloses insurance contracts (e.g., who the stop-loss carrier is, what the premium is); **Schedule C** discloses paid service providers (TPA, PBM, brokers) for larger plans. | This is **public data** — anyone can pull it in bulk from the DOL's EFAST2 system — and PDI uses it both to independently confirm facts already found in the documents, and (at scale) as a way to seed intelligence on plans the company hasn't been directly engaged to review yet. |
| **Stop-Loss Policy** | Separate insurance the employer buys to cap its own self-funded risk — a "specific" deductible (e.g., $75,000 per person) and an "aggregate" cap across the whole plan year. | It's a **separate contract with its own exclusions and definitions** — and mismatches between what the Plan Document promises and what the Stop-Loss Policy will actually reimburse create real, uncovered financial exposure ("gaps"). This is the same underlying risk our `../assessments/slgr/` documentation already covers in depth — PDI's version of this check is described in `04-deliverable-2-report-card.md` §D2-S6. |
| **ASA & vendor agreements** | The Administrative Services Agreement (the TPA contract) plus other vendor agreements (PBM, network, reference-based-pricing vendor, utilization management, navigation, etc.). | Collected and categorized by PDI, though not deeply parsed in this first version. |

## Plan-language concepts PDI has to recognize and grade

- **Subrogation / third-party recovery** — the plan's right to recover money it already paid out when a third party actually caused the injury (e.g., a car accident). The *strength* of this right depends on specific legal doctrine language being present: a first-priority equitable lien, an explicit disclaimer of the "made-whole doctrine" (otherwise the injured participant can argue the plan doesn't get paid back until *they've* been fully compensated first), a disclaimer of the "common-fund doctrine" (otherwise the participant's own attorney can deduct their fee from the plan's recovery share), the right to recover from the participant's own other coverage (like uninsured-motorist coverage), and the right to offset future benefits. Self-funded ERISA plans generally get federal law protection against state laws that would otherwise weaken this right — **but only if the plan document actually uses the right words.**
- **Out-of-network (OON) pricing methodology / RBP** — how the plan decides what to pay for care from a provider outside its network: negotiated network-adjacent rates, "usual, customary and reasonable" charges, or **reference-based pricing (RBP)** — typically a flat multiple of what Medicare would pay (e.g., "150% of the Medicare rate"). This choice directly drives how much a member could be balance-billed by the provider for the difference, and it's the anchor for one of the company's cross-sell services.
- **PACE (Plan Appointed Claim Evaluator)** — a service where the plan delegates its final-level internal appeal decisions to a specialized fiduciary. Whether a plan is even *eligible* for this is purely a language question: does the plan document name or permit a delegated claims fiduciary, grant that fiduciary real discretionary authority, and structure its appeals process in a compatible way?
- **NSA (No Surprises Act)** — the federal law on surprise medical billing: emergency services generally must be covered without prior authorization, at in-network cost-sharing levels, priced using a specific reference amount, with a formal dispute-resolution process available to providers. Plan documents drafted before this law took effect are frequently silent on it — a classic, easy-to-spot compliance gap.
- **Discretionary authority / "Firestone language"** — a specific legal doctrine question: does the plan document explicitly grant the plan administrator discretionary authority to interpret the plan and decide claims? If it does, courts generally review a benefit denial only for whether it was "arbitrary and capricious" (a deferential standard, favorable to the plan). If it doesn't, courts review the decision fresh, with no deference at all — a materially worse legal position for the plan sponsor. This single drafting choice is one of the most consequential compliance findings a review like this can surface.
- **Coordination of benefits (COB)**, including Medicare Secondary Payer rules, eligibility rules (waiting periods, hours requirements, leave-of-absence handling), and claims/appeals timing rules under ERISA — the standard regulatory-compliance surface any plan document review has to cover.

## Funding types PDI has to correctly distinguish — a single point of failure if gotten wrong

- **Self-funded** — employer bears the risk; the main case PDI is built for.
- **Fully insured** — an insurance carrier bears the risk; most of PDI's services and findings don't apply the same way, and the workflow treats these plans as largely out of scope (see `06-open-questions-and-risks.md`, OQ-3).
- **Level-funded** — a self-funded structure underneath, but bundled with stop-loss coverage and paid as fixed monthly amounts — it often *looks* self-funded on paper and can be genuinely indistinguishable from the documents alone.
- **Hybrid** arrangements.

**Why this is called out as a single point of failure, in the source specification's own words:** getting the funding type wrong "invalidates most downstream opportunity mapping." Because level-funded plans in particular can be impossible to tell apart from genuinely self-funded ones just by reading the documents, this is one of the few facts PDI is explicitly designed to *ask a human* about rather than infer — see `03-deliverable-1-intelligence-engine.md` §D1-S5.
