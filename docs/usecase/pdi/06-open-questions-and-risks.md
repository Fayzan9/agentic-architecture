# PDI — Open Questions & Business Risks

The source specification tracks ten explicit open questions ("OQ-1" through "OQ-10"), each with a named business owner and a stated working assumption to proceed on in the meantime. These are genuine, unresolved business decisions — not implementation details — so they're preserved here in full rather than summarized away, plus a few risks this document set adds based on cross-referencing against the real-world assessment work already documented in `../assessments/`.

## The ten open questions, as specified

| # | Question | Owner | Working assumption in the meantime |
|---|---|---|---|
| **OQ-1** | How is document data actually pulled from the two source systems (repository and authoring platform) — API, export, or manual pull? How often does it refresh? | IT / Implementation | Batch export for the initial pilot client; move to a live API connection later. |
| **OQ-2** | What exact thresholds (deficiency counts, document-age bands) and topic weights should drive the remediation-pathway recommendation? | Subject-matter experts | Ships with illustrative defaults, always behind human review, pending formal expert sign-off (a specific date was set for this ratification). |
| **OQ-3** | Are fully insured plans in scope for any version of the report card in v1? | Product / Sales | Assumption: detected and excluded from the standard path, with a defined message shown instead. |
| **OQ-4** | What calculation methodology and approved wording can actually be used for the quantified opportunity claims (e.g., "as much as 60%," "an estimated 20%")? | Subject-matter experts / Marketing / Legal | These claims go directly in front of clients and must be defensible — assumption is a legal/marketing-approved, deliberately hedged phrasing library. |
| **OQ-5** | What percentage of the client book actually has a stop-loss policy or TPA services agreement on file to work with? | Implementation team | Assumption: low initial coverage; the workflow is explicitly designed to degrade gracefully when this data isn't available. |
| **OQ-6** | When documents, the regulatory filing, and human-provided intake disagree with each other, which source wins? | Subject-matter experts / Implementation | Proposed default: documents > intake > filing for plan-design facts; intake wins for purely operational facts; disagreements are always shown, never hidden. |
| **OQ-7** | Who is the actual human reviewer of record for each topic, and how many approvals does a client-facing report need before it can go out? | Subject-matter expert leads | Assumption: a single approver per report, with the ability to escalate specific topics to a specialist. |
| **OQ-8** | When a Plan Document and SPD conflict with each other, is that always disclosed to the client, or can a reviewer choose to leave it out? | Subject-matter experts | Assumption: disclosed by default on the compliance page; a reviewer may rephrase it, but may not silently suppress it. |
| **OQ-9** | Should non-ERISA plans (church or municipal employer plans, which operate under a different legal framework entirely) be excluded, or given a reduced review? | Subject-matter experts | Assumption: flagged and excluded from the standard v1 path. |
| **OQ-10** | Who actually owns chasing down the answers to the implementation-intake questionnaire, and on what timeline? | Implementation team | Assumption: the implementation team owns it, with a 10-business-day service-level target. |

## Why these matter more than they might look like at first glance

Several of these open questions are not minor process details — they're decisions that materially affect the **legal defensibility and business risk profile** of a client-facing product:

- **OQ-4 (quantified claims) is the single highest-stakes item on this list.** A specific percentage claim like "may reduce spend by 60%" in a document a client's own broker or legal counsel will scrutinize is a real liability if the underlying methodology can't be defended. This is explicitly flagged in the source spec itself as needing legal sign-off before it ships — treat this as a hard blocker on report generation for any service opportunity claim, not a nice-to-have.
- **OQ-8 (conflict disclosure)** is a genuine tension between the product's core value proposition (surfacing real risk) and normal business incentives (a reviewer under pressure might prefer to soften an awkward finding). The stated assumption — disclosed by default, rephrasable but not suppressible — is the right default to preserve the product's credibility, and should be treated as a hard rule in the review-approval workflow, not just a convention.
- **OQ-6 (source precedence)** directly determines what the dashboard in `03-deliverable-1-intelligence-engine.md` §D1-S9 is actually allowed to claim with confidence. Getting this wrong doesn't just create one bad data point — it silently biases every rolled-up statistic built on top of it.

## Additional risks worth flagging, cross-referenced against the real-world assessment findings already documented

These aren't in the source specification directly, but follow directly from what the real client-sample validation in `../assessments/external_research/` already found about the underlying assessment content PDI is built on top of:

1. **Checklist content goes stale, and PDI inherits that risk at a larger scale.** The PDA cross-validation in `../assessments/external_research/pda-and-pdasl-analysis.md` found direct, real-world proof that checklist rationale can expire (a COVID-19-era provision, a HIPAA rule later vacated by a court). PDI's criteria library (§D2-S2) is the same kind of content, now doing more — driving not just a compliance finding, but an automated dollar-value sales claim and a specific remediation recommendation. **A stale criterion in PDI doesn't just produce a wrong finding — it can produce a wrong sales claim or push a client toward the wrong (and possibly more expensive) remediation path.** The criteria-library maintenance/refresh process implied by OQ-2's "expert ratification" needs to be a standing, recurring process, not a one-time launch activity.

2. **The Stop-Loss Gap logic (D2-S6) inherits SLGR's unresolved validation gap.** `../assessments/external_research/slgr-analysis.md` found that the standalone Stop Loss Gap Review has never been cross-checked against a real client deliverable, and that its "hard gap/soft gap" framing is not standardized industry terminology. PDI's version of this check should not be treated as more validated than the standalone product it's built on — if anything, it should be a **priority for real-sample validation before it starts driving automated dollar-exposure claims** in a client-facing report, given how much more consequential PDI's claims are (a specific dollar-exposure statement in an executive summary, not a caveated internal finding).

3. **The Schedule A "absence isn't evidence" problem is more subtle than the spec's own framing suggests.** The specification correctly notes that Schedule A discloses a plan's stop-loss carrier when present, and treats a Schedule A carrier disclosure as "a strong signal" of self-funded status. External research confirms an important nuance the spec doesn't call out explicitly: **Schedule A only requires disclosure of a stop-loss policy when it's held as a plan asset** (e.g., paid through a trust) — a very common structure where the employer pays stop-loss premiums from its own general assets will show **no stop-loss carrier on Schedule A at all**, even though the plan genuinely has stop-loss coverage. This means the *absence* of a Schedule A carrier entry must be treated the same cautious way the spec already treats a missing Form 5500 for a small plan — as inconclusive, not as evidence the plan lacks stop-loss coverage. Worth explicitly adding to the classification engine's (D1-S6) documented extraction logic for this field.

4. **The "we scanned your book and found X%" sales mechanic (D1-S9) is company framing, not a proven, externally-validated industry practice.** External research found real, independent confirmation that AI-driven proactive compliance scanning is a genuine and growing trend across the broader insurance/TPA technology space — but found no independent evidence that this *specific* sales pattern (an aggregate percentage-based "pre-conversation" pitch) is a documented practice used by named competitors. This doesn't mean the approach is wrong — it may well be a genuine differentiator — but it should be treated internally as **this company's own strategic bet**, not something to describe as "how everyone in the industry already does it."
