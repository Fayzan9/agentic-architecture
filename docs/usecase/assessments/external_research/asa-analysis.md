# ASA Review — External Validation Analysis

**Method:** the business-requirements document (`asa/README.md`, covering both the current-state approach and the target redesign) was checked against (1) the full text of the four reference-kit documents (Guide, Template, Troubling Provisions library, Addenda reference — all read in full), (2) independent external research into how the self-funded benefits industry actually reviews ASAs, and (3) **one real, complete client ASA review deliverable** (the actual contract plus the reviewer's real annotations on it, read in full). The client identity is not disclosed here; referred to as **Client C**.

## 1. What's CORRECT — strongly validated

### The 18-section structure and core topic areas are right
Reading the Guide in full confirms the current-state 18-section list (`prompts/asa.py`'s `LIST_OF_SECTIONS`) matches the Guide's own section-by-section structure almost exactly: Preamble/Recitals, Definitions, Financial Arrangements, Responsibilities of each party, Claims Processing, Compliance with Laws, Fiduciary Status, Liability Limitations, Confidentiality/Data Security, Termination, Dispute Resolution, Indemnification, Audit Rights, Performance Guarantees, Recent Regulatory Updates, Miscellaneous, Addenda. The Guide explicitly states its objective is to give reviewers "a detailed framework... incorporat[ing] insights from the [company's] ASA Template and recent regulatory updates" — confirming the Guide, Template, and Troubling Provisions library are meant to be used together, exactly as `asa/README.md`'s "reference kit" section describes.

### The regulatory scope is right, and independently corroborated with sharper specifics
External research confirms every regulatory area the Guide and current implementation target (ERISA fiduciary status, HIPAA/BAA, No Surprises Act/IDR, CAA fee disclosure, MHPAEA) is exactly what real-world ASA review practice covers — and adds concrete specifics worth folding into any rebuild:
- **Fiduciary delegation has a hard legal consequence, not just a drafting preference**: if a TPA is delegated claims/appeals authority, it *is* an ERISA fiduciary for that function regardless of a disclaimer — courts affirm fiduciaries cannot contract their way out of responsibility they've actually assumed. This directly validates the Troubling Provisions library's own annotation on a real carrier ASA: *"the disclaimer of fiduciary duties is not legally binding... if a TPA acts like a fiduciary, the TPA is a fiduciary."* The Guide, the Troubling Provisions library, and independent legal research all agree on this point.
- **Indemnification has a specific industry rule, not just "should be balanced"**: plan fiduciaries should not indemnify a TPA for the TPA's own negligence, misconduct, or fiduciary breach. The Template's actual indemnification language matches this (the Claims Administrator indemnifies the Plan Sponsor for its own "dishonest, fraudulent, grossly negligent, or criminal act," and vice versa) — but this is a concrete, checkable rule a rebuild's checklist should test for explicitly, not just "flag if indemnification looks one-sided."
- **BAA breach notification has a concrete number**: independent research cites a 60-day notification requirement; the Addenda reference's own BAA template specifies **10 days** for breach notice to the covered entity and **40 days** (not "without unreasonable delay" alone) for notice to affected individuals — worth reconciling which number is actually correct/current before treating either as ground truth in a rebuild, since they disagree.
- **CAA fee disclosure has a specific forward compliance date** (plan years beginning on/after a specific date in the near future per the research) worth version-checking against whatever the checklist encodes, since compliance-date-bearing content is exactly the kind of thing shown to go stale in the PDA analysis (see `pda-and-pdasl-analysis.md` §2).

### Cross-plan offsetting is real, litigated risk — not a hypothetical example
The Troubling Provisions library flags cross-plan offsetting (one carrier's plan recovering an overpayment by docking payments owed to an *unrelated* plan) as "extremely problematic," creating "major ERISA problems." External research independently confirms this is not a hypothetical: a federal district court held this practice violates ERISA fiduciary duty, the Department of Labor found a major carrier violated ERISA's exclusive-benefit and prohibited-transaction rules via this exact practice, and DOL reached a settlement with another carrier on the same theory in 2023. **This is real, enforced law, not a vendor's cautious opinion** — strong validation that this specific red flag belongs in any rebuild's fixed checklist or troubling-pattern library, at high severity.

### The Template's own drafting choices reveal real industry risk-allocation defaults worth encoding as checklist logic
Reading the Template in full surfaces concrete, checkable patterns not spelled out narratively in the Guide:
- The Template explicitly offers **two mutually exclusive fiduciary-status options** (Claims Administrator is / is not a fiduciary) as alternative preamble clauses — meaning "which option was selected" is itself a checkable fact a rebuild should extract, not just "is fiduciary status addressed."
- The Template's own liability/damages clause states the Claims Administrator is never liable for "special or consequential damages," and this is explicitly "controlling over any conflicting language in any agreement" — i.e., the Template itself models a common one-sided liability cap that a Plan Sponsor-side reviewer should specifically flag as a negotiation point, not just note as "present."
- The Template exposes a real **Section 3.6 either/or choice** on No Surprises Act responsibility: either the Claims Administrator actively handles NSA/IDR obligations, or it "explicitly assumes no responsibility" and offers a "separate written quote" for assistance. This is exactly the checklist item already identified in `asa/README.md`'s example items ("does the contract dump all NSA compliance on the sponsor without a corresponding service/fee") — confirmed as a real, drafted-in choice point in actual ASA templates, not a hypothetical.

## 2. What's a DOUBT or GAP

### The joint broker+counsel review process is understated
`asa/README.md` frames the review as "benefits counsel / consulting due diligence" performed by one reviewer. Independent research shows real-world practice is **explicitly joint**: employers typically rely on both a broker/consultant *and* legal counsel, since TPA selection itself is treated as a fiduciary act requiring a prudent process (often an RFP). This doesn't change the checklist content, but it suggests any rebuild's output framing ("negotiation memo for the party") should assume it may be read and acted on by a consultant as much as by counsel — worth keeping the deliverable's language accessible to a non-attorney reader, not just legally precise.

### The Addenda reference and Troubling Provisions library are real assets but weren't confirmed as actually used in the current implementation
`asa/README.md` already documents this as a known problem in the current-state approach ("Template / addenda / troubling provisions largely unused in the standalone path"), and reading the actual reference files in full only reinforces how much real, checkable content they contain that a naive "give the model the Guide" approach would miss: the Troubling Provisions library alone documents four different real carriers' (anonymized in this document) actual problematic clauses with specific legal reasoning per clause — this is exactly the kind of pattern-matching content that needs to be an active input to any Pass A/B design, not background material.

## 3. What's a CONFLICT — the most significant finding in this entire analysis

**The real ASA deliverable format does not match either the current implementation's output shape or the target redesign's documented finding schema.**

Reading the one available real ASA sample in full revealed that the actual delivered work product is **an inline-commented copy of the contract itself** (Microsoft Word comments attached to specific clauses, in context, throughout the actual agreement) — not a separate structured findings table with `topic` / `asa_contract_text` / `suggestion` / `criticality` columns (the current implementation's shape) and not the target design's structured `Finding` JSON schema (`section_name` / `topic_id` / `status` / `criticality` etc.) either.

Concrete observations from the real sample:
- **No explicit criticality (low/medium/high) labels appear anywhere.** Severity is conveyed entirely through the reviewer's word choice and framing (e.g., "You may want to add..." for a minor suggestion vs. "This appears to conflict with the language above..." for a genuine internal contradiction vs. "unusual, as most ASAs only require notice..." for a real negotiating point) — not a formal tag.
- **Comments are attached directly to the specific clause they concern**, inline in the document — a fundamentally different reading experience than a separate summary table.
- **Some comments are affirmative compliance confirmations, not just flags.** A cluster of comments on the contract's Business Associate Agreement section each individually confirm that a specific clause "satisfies [a named] HIPAA requirement" — i.e., the reviewer isn't only flagging problems, they're also documenting *why* specific compliant language is sufficient, clause by clause. This is a distinct annotation mode neither the current implementation nor the target design's schema explicitly accounts for (the target design's `status: present` covers "it's there" but not "and here specifically is which regulatory requirement it satisfies").
- **Genuine substantive findings do match the documented themes closely** — vague timeframes flagged for needing a specific number of days (matches the Guide's "specify timelines" best practice), an internal contradiction between two sections flagged explicitly, fee/recovery-timing ambiguity flagged (matches the Financial Arrangements theme), and one comment explicitly benchmarks against typical market practice ("unlike most ASAs, this agreement requires..." — direct evidence of the kind of Template/market comparison `asa/README.md`'s target design calls for), so the *substance* of the review is well-aligned with the documented business rules even though the *output format* is not.

**What this means for a rebuild:** before building either the current-state shell or the target Pass A/B schema as the literal output format, **a real design decision is needed on deliverable shape** — inline document annotation (redline/comment style, matching what this real sample shows clients actually receive) versus a separate structured report (matching PDA's actual real-world format, and the target design's schema). These are genuinely different products from a user-experience standpoint, and the one real ASA sample available suggests the actual expectation, at least for this reviewer/client relationship, was the former. This should be explicitly decided, not defaulted to whichever is easier to generate from a JSON schema.

### The "two-pass" (Pass A checklist + Pass B discovery) methodology is a reasonable engineering decomposition, not documented professional practice
Independent research into general contract-review methodology confirms legal/procurement reviewers do use both a documented "playbook" (checklist-equivalent) *and* open issue-spotting — but **no source frames these as two formally sequential, separate passes.** In practice, human reviewers blend playbook-matching and issue-spotting continuously in one read-through, exactly as the real Client C sample shows (compliance-confirmation comments and issue-flagging comments interleaved throughout the same document, not separated into two phases). **Frame Pass A/Pass B internally as this project's own AI-system engineering choice for reliability and coverage** — a legitimate design decision, but not something to describe as "how ASA counsel actually works," since that overstates what the research supports.

## 4. Summary of required changes / decisions needed

1. **Decide the actual deliverable format before building further**: inline contract annotation vs. a separate structured report. The one real sample available points toward inline annotation; this conflicts with both documented implementations' assumed output shape.
2. If a structured `Finding` schema is still used internally (e.g., to drive downstream automation, gold-eval comparison, or a structured UI), **add a "compliance confirmation" finding type** alongside "issue" findings — the real sample shows reviewers actively document *why* a clause is sufficient, not just flag what's wrong.
3. Treat "criticality: low/medium/high" as a system-imposed structuring device, not something the real underlying review process labels explicitly today — this is fine to keep for a rebuild's own consistency, but shouldn't be described as reflecting real reviewer behavior.
4. Reconcile the BAA breach-notification timing discrepancy (60 days per general industry research vs. 10/40 days in the Addenda reference's actual template) before encoding either as a fixed checklist answer.
5. Encode the Template's real either/or decision points (fiduciary status option, NSA responsibility option) as extractable facts a rebuild should identify from the contract, not just narrative topics to discuss.
6. Ensure the Troubling Provisions library's real, cited-carrier content is an active input to whatever pattern-matching mechanism a rebuild uses — not background context a model might ignore, which is the exact failure already documented for the current implementation.
